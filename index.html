How it works

This tool estimates how much sugar (water-soluble carbohydrate) your grass is likely to be carrying now and over the week ahead, then scales that by how susceptible your horse is. It reads live weather for your location, works out a grass-sugar score, and turns it into a 1–100 risk figure. It does not measure your grass or your horse — it infers risk from the weather patterns known to drive grass sugar.

What it uses

  • Weather — Open-Meteo (free, no account): daily high and low temperature, sunshine hours, day length and rainfall, plus the current temperature, for the last 7 days and the 7 days ahead, in your local time.
  • Location — UK postcodes via postcodes.io (a full postcode, or just the area code like CW4), any place name via Open-Meteo, or your device's location.
  • Your horse — the settings you choose, saved in your browser on this device so they're ready next time.

Why weather predicts grass sugar

Grass makes sugar by day through photosynthesis and burns it off through respiration — but respiration roughly stops below 5 °C. So the classic danger is a cold or frosty night followed by a bright day: sugar is produced but not used, and it builds up. Drought stress and a flush of new growth after rain also push sugars up.

The daily grass score (0–100)

Every day, past or forecast, gets a score built from two main parts plus adjustments:

score = 0.45 × night factor + 0.40 × sunshine factor + adjustments
Overnight lowNight factor
Below 0 °C (frost)100

The sunshine factor is the share of daylight that was sunny (sunshine hours ÷ day length), scaled 0–100. Then two bonuses: +15 when a frosty night (below 2 °C) meets a bright day, and +5 on a cold, dull-growth day (high below 8 °C with some sun). The total is capped at 100.

The "now" figure

It starts from the recent grass score (today weighted 70%, yesterday 30%), then applies:

  • Rainfall over 7 days — +8 if very dry (under 5 mm, drought stress), and +8 for a dry-then-wet growth flush.
  • Time of day — a factor from about 0.85 near 5 am (sugars lowest) up to 1.05 near 4 pm (highest). After a cold night (low below 5 °C) it never dips below 1.0, because sugars don't fall overnight then.
  • Your horse — the multiplier below.
now risk = grass level × time-of-day × horse multiplier  (shown 1–100)

Today, hour by hour

The hourly chart takes the same grass level as the gauge and shapes it across 24 hours with that time-of-day factor, marking the current hour. On a mild day you'll see a low-morning, afternoon-peak curve; after a cold night the line stays flat and high — the sign that the early-morning turnout window isn't safe that day.

Last 7 days & forecast

Each day's grass score is multiplied by your horse's factor and plotted for the past week and the week ahead, split by the "today" line. The dashed line is the grass-only (weather) score, for comparison.

Adjusting for your horse

Each choice adds or removes points; the total becomes a multiplier of 1 + points ÷ 100, held between ×0.3 and ×1.8. An average horse is ×1.00. The horse's own metabolism matters more than the weather, which is why these can move the number so much.

What the bands mean

1–25Low — normal grazing is fine for most horses.
26–50Moderate — watch at-risk horses; shorten turnout or muzzle.
51–75High — restrict grazing for at-risk horses.
76–100Very high — keep laminitis-prone horses off grass.

Limits — please read

  • No public source measures actual grass sugar; this is an inference from weather, not a measurement. Forage sugar testing is the gold standard for a known at-risk horse.
  • The thresholds and weights are informed estimates, not calibrated against laminitis outcome data.
  • The real-world driver is the horse's own metabolism (insulin dysregulation); the app can only approximate susceptibility from what you tell it.
  • Guidance only, not veterinary advice. If a horse shows signs of laminitis, or you're managing a diagnosed metabolic horse, speak to your vet.
